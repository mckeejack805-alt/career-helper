How the fit score works

Your major decides which careers you see. Each career lists the majors that usually lead into it, plus related majors that can also get there. Careers outside your major are hidden unless you turn them on.

Each career also has a profile: where it sits on the three work-preference scales (1 to 5) and the interests and skills it rewards most.

A student's match is a weighted blend of four parts. Preferences measures how close their three slider answers are to the career's scores. Interests and Skills measure overlap between what they picked and what the career rewards (picking everything doesn't help, since extra picks dilute the score). Major fit is full credit for a typical major, partial credit for a related major, and low credit for careers outside the major.

Year in school doesn't change the match. It changes the next-step advice shown with the top result.

The fit score is a guide built from career profiles that are a first draft. They're the main thing to refine once real students try the app.

Where the pay numbers come from

Starting pay is the average of two or three published sources for new graduates in each role, listed below. Job-board salary data (ZipRecruiter, Payscale) varies by employer and city, so treat each figure as a typical starting point, not a promise.

Around year 5 is the average of two or three sources for the job title people typically hold about five years in. For investment banking and private equity it is total pay including bonus, since bonuses make up most of the pay at that stage.

"Earn at least" (the share of workers earning at least the bottom of your salary range) comes from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) Occupational Outlook Handbook, using 2025 pay for the closest matching occupation. It covers all workers in that occupation at every experience level, and is estimated from the BLS 10th, 50th, and 90th percentile wages.

Model check (for app builders)

Runs random students through the fit score to check that no career gets recommended far too often or too rarely. Students don't need this.

Simulate students

Generates random students in one major, runs each through the matching model, and shows which careers the model recommends most. Use it to spot a model that is biased toward one career.
